Key Findings

Key Findings: Sizing a Home Generator in Texas

Texas homeowners face a generator sizing problem unlike most of the country. ERCOT's grid carried roughly 85,500 MW of demand during the August 2023 heat dome, and the February 2021 Winter Storm Uri left 4.5 million Texas households without power for an average of 42 hours. Sizing decisions made for "occasional outages" elsewhere become life-safety decisions here.

The 50% rule misleads Texas buyers. National sizing guides often recommend covering half a home's connected load. In Texas, central air conditioning is not optional – interior temperatures in a 2,000 sq ft uninsulated attic-vented home can exceed 95°F within four hours of AC loss, per a 2022 Texas A&M AgriLife study. Our calculator weights HVAC as a non-negotiable load rather than a discretionary one.

  • 3-ton central AC: 3,500 W running / 10,500 W starting (LRA surge)
  • 4-ton central AC: 4,800 W running / 14,400 W starting
  • Electric water heater: 4,500 W
  • Well pump (common in rural counties): 1,000–2,000 W running / 3× starting
  • Refrigerator + chest freezer: 800 W combined
  • Lighting, outlets, internet: 600 W

A 3-ton AC home with gas water heat and gas range typically needs a 14–18 kW standby unit. All-electric homes – increasingly common in master-planned developments around Austin, San Antonio, and DFW – push requirements to 22–26 kW. Homes with two AC zones, EV charging, or pool pumps frequently require 27–36 kW.

Fuel matters more in Texas than the brochures admit. Natural gas service is widespread in metro areas but absent across much of the Hill Country and East Texas. Propane standby units lose roughly 10–12% rated output above 95°F ambient, per Generac's spec sheets – a 22 kW unit effectively delivers 19.4 kW during a July afternoon outage. Our calculator applies a derate factor based on ZIP code climate zone.

Starting watts are where homeowners undersize. Air conditioner compressors draw 3× their running wattage for 200–400 milliseconds at startup. A generator sized to running load will trip on the first AC cycle. Soft-start kits (around $350 installed) reduce LRA by 60–75% and can drop required generator size by one tier, often paying for themselves versus upsizing.

Texas Electrical Code (NEC 2023, adopted statewide January 2024) requires a manual or automatic transfer switch for any permanently installed generator over 5 kW.

What It Means for Homeowners

Texas homeowners face a generator sizing decision shaped by the state's unique grid pressures. After Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 left roughly 4.5 million households without power – some for days – and following ERCOT's repeated conservation appeals during the 2023 and 2024 heat domes, standby generation has shifted from luxury to risk management. Sizing it correctly is where most projects go wrong.

A whole-home generator for an average 2,000–2,500 sq ft Texas house typically requires 20–26 kW, driven largely by central air conditioning. A 4-ton AC unit alone can pull 6,000–8,000 starting watts, and in a climate where Houston, Dallas, and Austin routinely exceed 100°F, cooling cannot be treated as a deferrable load. Undersizing – a common error when homeowners use generic online calculators – causes nuisance shutdowns, voltage sag that damages variable-speed HVAC compressors, and voided manufacturer warranties.

Practical takeaways: inventory your largest motor loads (AC, well pump, pool pump) and use locked-rotor amps, not running watts. Account for simultaneous startup. If you have solar, confirm the generator integrates with your existing transfer switch and any battery system. Natural gas units derate roughly 10–15% in extreme heat, so build in headroom.

Call a licensed Texas electrician – TDLR-licensed, per state law – before purchase, not after. They will pull a manual load calculation, verify gas line capacity (a frequent bottleneck), and file the permit your municipality requires. A correctly sized 22 kW install runs $12,000–$18,000; a wrongly sized one costs that plus replacement.

Texas homeowners lost power an average of 20 hours during 2021's Winter Storm Uri, per ERCOT data. Sizing your backup generator correctly – typically 7,500 to 12,000 watts for essential home circuits – prevents overload failures when you need power most. Data & methodology

Generator sizing recommendations on this page are calculated using the National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 220 load calculation method, cross-referenced with appliance wattage data published by the U.S. Department of Energy and manufacturer specifications from Generac, Kohler, and Cummins. Texas-specific load assumptions reflect ERCOT residential consumption data, which shows the average Texas home consumes roughly 1,174 kWh per month – about 32% higher than the national average, driven largely by cooling demand. Air conditioning load estimates assume a 3- to 5-ton central system, the standard sizing range for Texas homes between 1,500 and 2,500 square feet according to the Texas A&M Energy Systems Laboratory.

Starting wattage multipliers for inductive loads (compressors, well pumps, pool equipment) follow NEMA MG-1 standards, typically 2.5x to 3x running wattage. Climate adjustment factors incorporate Texas Climate Zone 2 and 3 designations from the 2021 International Energy Conservation Code, as adopted by the Texas State Energy Conservation Office. Calculations assume single-phase 240V residential service, the standard configuration for Oncor, CenterPoint, and AEP Texas service territories.
