how to Size Backup Generator for Home

Sizing a backup generator for a Texas home starts with auditing actual load, not guessing wattage. After Winter Storm Uri left 4.5 million ERCOT customers dark in February 2021, demand for a backup power generator for house use surged statewide. This insight breaks down how to calculate starting and running watts for HVAC, well pumps, and refrigeration, why a 50 amp generator suits most 2,000–3,000 sq ft homes, and how transfer switch capacity dictates whether you need a portable electric generator or the right home standby generator in your budget. Key takeaway: Texas homeowners on slab foundations with electric heat pumps typically need 18–26 kW standby units, while gas-heated homes can run essential circuits on a back generator for home use rated 7,500–12,000 watts. Right-size before you shop.

Key Findings

Here is what the numbers actually show.

The 50-amp threshold is the practical floor for whole-home coverage. A 50 amp generator delivers roughly 12,000 watts on a 240V circuit, which aligns with the median continuous load of a 2,000–2,500 sq ft Texas home running central AC, refrigeration, well pump, and essential lighting simultaneously. Winter Storm Uri (February 2021), the May 2024 Houston derecho, and Hurricane Beryl (July 2024) each left many Texas homes without power for days – long enough that partial-load "essentials only" rigs can leave families without cooling during 95°F+ summer events, when indoor temperatures climb quickly.

Air conditioning is the single largest sizing variable. A 3-ton central AC unit pulls 5,000–7,000 starting watts and 3,500–4,000 running watts. Homeowners searching for a "back generator for home" or "back up generator for house" often default to 7,500W portables, which cannot start a typical Texas AC compressor without a soft-start kit. Adding a soft starter (often roughly $300–$500 installed) can sharply reduce an AC compressor's starting current, frequently the difference between a workable 9,000W unit and a required 14,000W unit.

  • 14kW: Covers AC, kitchen, lighting, and essential outlets for homes under 2,000 sq ft.
  • 18–20kW: The most common Texas install, handling two AC zones plus electric range or dryer (not both simultaneously).
  • 22–24kW: Often needed for 3,000+ sq ft homes, dual HVAC, or electric water heating.
  • 26kW+: All-electric homes, pool equipment, EV charging, or shop loads.

Fuel source shifts the math. Natural gas standby units are usually rated somewhat lower than on propane – a Generac 22 kW unit is rated 22 kW on propane and about 19.5–21 kW on natural gas depending on model – which matters when sizing against summer peak load. Propane reserves of 250+ gallons are recommended for the 72-hour outage scenario Texas emergency management now models as plausible.

Bottom line: a portable electric generator under 9,000W is a refrigerator-and-fan solution, not a Texas-summer solution. For a true backup power generator for house-wide resilience, target a permanently installed standby unit between 18kW and 24kW, sized against your measured peak rather than nameplate ratings.

What It Means for Homeowners

For Texas homeowners, generator sizing has shifted from a comfort decision to a resilience calculation. After Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 left more than 4.5 million Texas homes and businesses without power, followed by major outages from the May 2024 Houston derecho and Hurricane Beryl in July 2024, many homeowners started looking at backup power. That pattern reframes what a backup power generator for a house actually needs to do.

Practically, sizing starts with load math, not marketing tiers. A typical 2,000–2,500 sq ft Texas home with central AC, well pump, refrigeration, and a few essential circuits draws 18–22 kW under realistic summer conditions. Searches for "50 amp generator" often reflect RV-style portable units around 12 kW – enough for partial-load transfer switches, but undersized for whole-home cooling during a 100°F afternoon. The right home standby generator for most Texas houses falls between 20 kW and 26 kW on natural gas, or 22–24 kW on propane given lower BTU density.

Takeaways: inventory starting watts (AC compressors can draw several times their running current at startup), confirm fuel supply – natural gas service continued in many areas during Uri, while propane tanks lose pressure in severe cold, so keep them well filled – and choose a unit rated for humidity and salt air if you live near the Gulf Coast.

Call a licensed Texas electrician before purchase, not after. They'll perform an electrical load calculation (NEC Articles 220 and 702), confirm service-panel compatibility (200A is standard but not universal), and pull the required municipal permit. Self-installed transfer switches remain a leading cause of homeowner electrocution and insurance claim denial statewide, per TDLR incident reporting.

Sizing a backup generator for your house isn't guesswork – Texas heat loads, well pumps, and 50-amp circuits all shift the math.
