Key Findings

Key Findings: Sizing Your Texas Electrical Panel

Texas homeowners are upgrading electrical panels at the highest rate in a generation, and the math behind those decisions follows a well-defined federal standard. The National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 220 governs how load calculations must be performed, and every licensed Texas electrician – regulated by the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) – is required to follow it before pulling a permit.

The 100-amp panel is no longer the default. According to U.S. Census Bureau data, the median new Texas home built since 2020 exceeds 2,300 square feet, and roughly 61% include electric ranges, electric dryers, or both. Under NEC's standard method, a 2,400 sq ft all-electric home with central HVAC, a 5 kW range, a 5 kW dryer, and a water heater typically calculates to 150–175 amps of demand. That places it firmly in 200-amp service territory – the size now standard in nearly all new Texas residential construction.

Three Texas-specific factors drive panels even larger. First, ERCOT summer peak loads regularly push 5-ton central air conditioners (running 30–40 amps continuous) for 8+ hours a day, and NEC requires the largest motor load to be counted at 125%. Second, EV adoption is climbing: Texas registered over 230,000 EVs by 2025, and a Level 2 charger adds a continuous 40–48 amp circuit that must be included in load calculations. Third, post-Winter Storm Uri (2021), backup generator and battery interlocks – now installed in roughly 1 in 7 new Texas homes – require dedicated panel capacity and often a service upgrade to integrate safely.

When the calculator says you're over 80%, you're already behind. NEC 220.83 caps continuous loads at 80% of panel rating. A 200-amp panel pulling a calculated 165 amps is at its practical ceiling. Adding a hot tub, a pool pump, a workshop subpanel, or an EV charger to a home already near that threshold almost always triggers a service upgrade – typically a 320/400-amp meter base in Texas, which has become common in new builds in Austin, Houston, and Dallas-Fort Worth.

Bottom line: A modern Texas home with central AC, electric appliances, and any EV or solar plans should calculate at minimum 200 amps. Homes over 3,500 sq ft, or those with workshops, pools, or dual HVAC systems, frequently require 320 or 400 amps. Use the calculator above with your actual appliance nameplate ratings – not estimates – for a code-compliant result.

What It Means for Homeowners

Texas homeowners face a distinct calculus when sizing an electrical panel. The state's residential building code references the 2020 National Electrical Code, which requires service-load calculations under Article 220 before any panel upgrade is permitted. For most single-family homes built after 2000, a 200-amp panel has become the default – but ERCOT data shows average Texas household electricity use runs roughly 1,174 kWh per month, about 30% above the national average, driven largely by summer cooling loads that can push a 3-ton central AC alone to draw 15–20 amps continuously.

What this means in practice: if your home still runs on a 100-amp service, adding an EV charger (typically 40–50 amps), a heat pump, or a pool pump can exceed safe capacity. A calculator estimate of 160 amps or higher on a 100-amp panel is a clear upgrade signal. Homes in Austin, Houston, and Dallas neighborhoods built before 1980 frequently still have 60- or 100-amp service paired with aluminum branch wiring – a combination flagged by the CPSC as a fire risk.

Call a licensed Texas electrician (TDLR-licensed, verifiable at tdlr.texas.gov) when your calculated load exceeds 80% of panel capacity, when breakers trip repeatedly under normal use, or before installing any 240-volt appliance. Panel upgrades in Texas typically run $2,000–$4,500 and require both a municipal permit and utility coordination for meter disconnection. DIY panel work is illegal for non-licensed individuals under Texas Occupations Code Chapter 1305 and will void homeowner's insurance claims tied to electrical fires.

Texas homes built before 1985 often run 100-amp service – insufficient for modern HVAC, EV chargers, and induction ranges. The 2023 NEC requires load calculations before any panel upgrade. Data & methodology

Panel size recommendations on this calculator follow the load calculation methods specified in the National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 220, which Texas adopts statewide through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation (TDLR) under 16 TAC §73.100. Most Texas jurisdictions currently enforce the 2020 NEC, though Austin, Houston, and Dallas have amendments adopting the 2023 edition with local modifications.

Square-footage-based general lighting loads (3 VA per square foot) and standard appliance demand factors are applied per NEC 220.42 and 220.53. HVAC load assumptions reflect Texas climate realities: cooling-dominant calculations using ERCOT-region summer design temperatures from ASHRAE Fundamentals, since air conditioning typically drives Texas residential peak demand to 4–6 kW for a 2,000 sq ft home, according to ERCOT's 2024 residential load profile data.

EV charger inputs default to Level 2 (40A/9.6 kW) loads, reflecting adoption trends reported by the Texas Comptroller's office. Solar and battery interconnection allowances follow NEC 705.12 busbar rules. Always confirm final sizing with a TDLR-licensed master electrician before permitting.
