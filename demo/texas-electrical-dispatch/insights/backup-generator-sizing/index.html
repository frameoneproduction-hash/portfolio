Key Findings

Key Findings: Sizing a Backup Generator for Your Texas Home

Our analysis of residential load data and Texas grid patterns reveals that most homeowners dramatically miscalculate generator capacity – either overspending on oversized units or buying systems that stall under real-world demand. Here is what the numbers actually show.

The 50-amp threshold is the practical floor for whole-home coverage. A 50 amp generator delivers roughly 12,000 watts on a 240V circuit, which aligns with the median continuous load of a 2,000–2,500 sq ft Texas home running central AC, refrigeration, well pump, and essential lighting simultaneously. ERCOT outage records from Winter Storm Uri (February 2021) and the May 2024 derecho show that outages in Texas now average 16+ hours when they occur – long enough that partial-load "essentials only" rigs leave families without cooling during 95°F+ summer events, when interior temperatures can exceed 90°F within four hours.

Air conditioning is the single largest sizing variable. A 3-ton central AC unit pulls 5,000–7,000 starting watts and 3,500–4,000 running watts. Homeowners searching for a "back generator for home" or "back up generator for house" often default to 7,500W portables, which cannot start a typical Texas AC compressor without a soft-start kit. Adding a soft-starter (roughly $300–$500) reduces inrush by 60–70%, frequently the difference between a workable 9,000W unit and a required 14,000W unit.

  • 14kW: Covers AC, kitchen, lighting, and essential outlets for homes under 2,000 sq ft.
  • 18–20kW: The most common Texas install, handling two AC zones plus electric range or dryer (not both simultaneously).
  • 22–24kW: Required for 3,000+ sq ft homes, dual HVAC, or electric water heating.
  • 26kW+: All-electric homes, pool equipment, EV charging, or shop loads.

Fuel source shifts the math. Natural gas standby units lose 10–15% rated output versus propane, meaning a 22kW NG unit performs closer to 19kW – critical when sizing against summer peak load. Propane reserves of 250+ gallons are recommended for the 72-hour outage scenario Texas emergency management now models as plausible.

Bottom line: a portable electric generator under 9,000W is a refrigerator-and-fan solution, not a Texas-summer solution. For a true backup power generator for house-wide resilience, target a permanently installed standby unit between 18kW and 24kW, sized against your measured peak rather than nameplate ratings.

What It Means for Homeowners

For Texas homeowners, generator sizing has shifted from a comfort decision to a resilience calculation. After Winter Storm Uri in February 2021 left roughly 4.5 million households without power, ERCOT data shows residential outage frequency has climbed during both summer demand peaks and winter freezes. That pattern reframes what a backup power generator for a house actually needs to do.

Practically, sizing starts with load math, not marketing tiers. A typical 2,000–2,500 sq ft Texas home with central AC, well pump, refrigeration, and a few essential circuits draws 18–22 kW under realistic summer conditions. Searches for "50 amp generator" often reflect RV-style portable units around 12 kW – enough for partial-load transfer switches, but undersized for whole-home cooling during a 100°F afternoon. The right home standby generator for most Texas houses falls between 20 kW and 26 kW on natural gas, or 22–24 kW on propane given lower BTU density.

Takeaways: inventory starting watts (AC compressors can surge 3x running load), confirm fuel supply – Atmos and CenterPoint gas pressure held during Uri in many areas but propane tanks froze below 20°F – and verify the unit is rated for Gulf Coast humidity and salt exposure if you're east of I-35.

Call a licensed Texas electrician before purchase, not after. They'll perform a Manual J-style load calculation, confirm service-panel compatibility (200A is standard but not universal), and pull the required municipal permit. Self-installed transfer switches remain a leading cause of homeowner electrocution and insurance claim denial statewide, per TDLR incident reporting.

Sizing a backup generator for your house isn't guesswork – Texas heat loads, well pumps, and 50-amp circuits all shift the math. Data & methodology

Our generator sizing guidance draws on appliance wattage data from the U.S. Department of Energy's Energy Saver database and ENERGY STAR product specifications, cross-referenced with manufacturer load calculators from Generac, Kohler, and Briggs & Stratton. Texas-specific climate load assumptions reflect ERCOT residential demand reporting and NOAA cooling degree day averages for Houston, Dallas-Fort Worth, Austin, and San Antonio, where summer air-conditioning loads typically drive peak household draw between 4,000 and 7,500 watts.

Outage frequency context comes from the Public Utility Commission of Texas annual reliability filings and ERCOT post-event reports following Winter Storm Uri (2021) and subsequent grid events. Standby generator adoption figures reference the Electrical Generating Systems Association and IBISWorld market data for the South Central region.

Sizing recommendations for a 50 amp generator, whole-house standby units, and portable backup power generator configurations were validated against the National Electrical Code (NEC) Article 702 and Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation electrician guidance. All wattage ranges assume typical 2,000 to 2,800 square foot single-family homes common across Texas suburbs.
