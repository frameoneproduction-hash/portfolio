Complete Outdoor Lighting Guide

This guide examines outdoor lighting systems for Texas residential properties, covering fixture selection, voltage options, photocell and timer controls, weatherproofing standards, and code compliance under the 2023 National Electrical Code as adopted across Texas jurisdictions. It analyzes low-voltage landscape lighting versus 120-volt line systems, LED efficiency gains, dark-sky considerations relevant to municipalities like Dripping Springs and Horseshoe Bay, and GFCI requirements for outdoor circuits mandated by NEC Article 210.8. Installation costs, permit thresholds, and licensed electrician requirements under Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation rules are also addressed. Key takeaway: Texas homeowners should prioritize wet-location-rated fixtures, GFCI-protected circuits, and licensed electrician installation for any 120-volt work. Properly designed low-voltage systems deliver substantial energy savings while meeting local ordinances, insurance requirements, and the state's intense heat, humidity, and storm exposure conditions.

Key Findings

Key Findings: Outdoor Lighting in Texas Homes

Outdoor lighting has shifted from a luxury upgrade to a baseline expectation for Texas homeowners, and the data behind that shift is striking. According to the National Association of Home Builders' "What Home Buyers Really Want" study, exterior lighting consistently ranks as the most-wanted outdoor feature, with roughly 9 in 10 buyers calling it essential or desirable. In Texas, where average lot sizes (0.19 acres statewide per Census ACS data) exceed national medians and outdoor living seasons stretch eight to ten months a year, that demand translates into real installation activity.

Energy and cost data favor LED conversion. The U.S. Department of Energy estimates LED fixtures use 75% less energy and last up to 25 times longer than incandescent equivalents. For a typical Texas home running 10 exterior fixtures four hours nightly, switching from 60W halogens to 9W LEDs saves roughly $90–$110 annually at the statewide average residential rate of 14.4¢/kWh (EIA, 2025). Over a fixture lifespan, that's $1,500+ in avoided energy and replacement costs per home.

Safety and security gains are measurable. A peer-reviewed study published by the Crime Lab at the University of Chicago (2019) found that improved outdoor lighting reduced reported nighttime outdoor crimes by 36% in treated areas. Insurance carriers including State Farm and Allstate, both major Texas underwriters, document lighting as a qualifying factor for home protective-device premium reductions of 2–5%.

Climate dictates fixture selection. Texas spans USDA hardiness zones 6b through 10a and four distinct climate regions. Coastal homeowners from Houston to Corpus Christi need fixtures rated for salt-spray corrosion (look for marine-grade brass or 316 stainless). West Texas installations face UV degradation and 100°F+ ambient heat that shortens driver life in sealed fixtures – IP65-rated housings with thermal venting outperform sealed plastics. Hill Country and North Texas properties contend with hail; impact-rated polycarbonate lenses survive where glass fails.

Dark-sky compliance is now a regulatory factor. Eleven Texas municipalities, including Dripping Springs (the state's first International Dark Sky Community), Wimberley, Fort Davis, and Horseshoe Bay, enforce ordinances capping color temperature at 3000K and requiring full-cutoff shielding. Even outside these jurisdictions, HOAs increasingly mirror the standards.

Voltage choice shapes scope and permitting. Low-voltage (12V) landscape systems install without an electrical permit under the 2023 NEC as adopted by Texas; line-voltage (120V) work requires a licensed electrician under TDLR rules and pulled permits in most jurisdictions.

What It Means for Homeowners

What This Means for Texas Homeowners

Outdoor lighting in Texas operates under conditions most national guides underestimate. Summer ambient temperatures regularly exceed 100°F, and fixture housings in direct sun can reach 140°F or higher – well above the 25°C rating most manufacturers use for lumen and lifespan claims. Expect LED drivers rated for 40°C ambient to underperform or fail prematurely on south- and west-facing walls. Look for fixtures rated to 50°C ambient and wet-location listings, particularly along the Gulf Coast where humidity and salt air accelerate corrosion on standard aluminum housings.

The practical takeaways are straightforward. Low-voltage (12V) landscape systems remain the safest DIY path for path lights, uplights, and tree wash, provided the transformer is GFCI-protected and sized with 20% headroom for voltage drop on Texas-sized lots. Line-voltage fixtures, anything mounted on the home's exterior envelope, and any wiring buried more than a few inches require permitted work in most Texas jurisdictions – Austin, Dallas, Houston, and San Antonio all require licensed electricians for new exterior circuits under the 2020 NEC, which Texas adopted statewide.

Call a licensed electrician when the project involves a new dedicated circuit, panel work, mounting fixtures on stucco or masonry, trenching across irrigation or gas lines, or integrating lighting with smart panels and EV chargers. Also call one if existing outdoor receptacles lack weather-resistant (WR) and tamper-resistant (TR) markings, or if GFCI protection trips repeatedly – both indicate code-compliance gaps that DIY fixture swaps will not solve and that insurance carriers increasingly scrutinize after claims.

Texas homeowners invest an average of $2,000–$4,500 on outdoor lighting installations, according to HomeAdvisor data.
