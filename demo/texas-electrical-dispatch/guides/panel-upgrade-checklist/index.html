Electrical Panel Upgrade Checklist

Upgrading an electrical panel in Texas is increasingly common as homes built before the 1990s push past the 100-amp service capacity that modern HVAC, EV chargers, and induction appliances routinely demand. This checklist walks homeowners through the decision points: verifying panel amperage and bus condition, identifying recalled brands like Federal Pacific and Zinsco still found in older Houston, Dallas, and Austin housing stock, confirming permit requirements with the local Authority Having Jurisdiction, and coordinating the temporary disconnect with Oncor, CenterPoint, or AEP Texas. It also covers grounding upgrades required under the 2026 NEC, which Texas adopted statewide effective September 1, 2026 (cities may add local amendments). Key takeaway: a compliant 200-amp upgrade typically runs $2,500–$4,500 in Texas metros, and skipping the utility coordination step is a common cause of delays and failed inspections.

Key Findings

Texas homeowners considering an electrical panel upgrade face a convergence of cost, code, and grid-reliability pressures that make 2026 an unusually consequential year to get the decision right. Upgrades to 320- or 400-amp service – increasingly common for homes adding EV chargers, heat pumps, or backup batteries – typically run $4,500 to $8,000.

Code compliance is the most common driver of failed inspections. Texas adopted the 2026 National Electrical Code effective September 1, 2026, and most major jurisdictions including Austin, San Antonio, and Houston now enforce it. Key checklist items include: AFCI/GFCI protection on all required branch circuits (NEC 210.12 and 210.8), an emergency disconnect in a readily accessible outdoor location for one- and two-family dwellings (NEC 230.85), a surge protective device at the service equipment (NEC 230.67), and proper labeling of grounding electrode conductors.

Permit data tells the rest of the story. ERCOT's long-term forecasts project rapid growth in statewide peak demand, driven largely by large new loads such as data centers and industrial projects, and home electrification adds its own pressure – so older 100-amp services can struggle with modern cooling and appliance loads.

A defensible homeowner checklist should verify: a TDLR-licensed electrical contractor with licensed electricians doing the work (searchable at tdlr.texas.gov), a pulled permit and scheduled inspection, service-entrance conductors (copper or aluminum) sized to the new ampacity, the neutral-to-ground bond made at the main service panel only, a meter base that meets the local utility's specs (Oncor, CenterPoint, AEP, and CPS Energy requirements vary), and written warranty terms for both the panel and the labor.

What It Means for Homeowners

What This Means for Texas Homeowners

If your home was built before 1990, your electrical panel is statistically near or past its service life. Electrical panels commonly last several decades, and roughly one in three Texas housing units was built before 1980, according to U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey data. That matters because modern Texas households draw far more current than panels were sized for a generation ago – a single Level 2 EV charger pulls 40 to 50 amps, and variable-speed HVAC systems sized for 100-degree summers routinely demand 30-plus amps on their own circuit.

Practical takeaways: Walk your checklist before summer peak load hits ERCOT's grid. Look for the documented red flags – Federal Pacific Stab-Lok or Zinsco panels (both widely reported for breakers that may fail to trip), rust at the meter base, double-tapped breakers, or a 60- to 100-amp main when your appliances suggest you need 200. Photograph the panel label and breaker layout before requesting quotes; reputable Texas contractors will give a firmer estimate from clear photos than from a phone description.

When to call a licensed electrician: immediately if you smell burning plastic, see scorch marks, or breakers trip on routine loads. Schedule a non-urgent evaluation if your panel is over 25 years old, you're planning solar, an EV charger, or a heat pump conversion, or your homeowner's insurance carrier has flagged the panel brand. Texas requires a licensed master or journeyman electrician for panel work under TDLR rules – DIY panel swaps void coverage and violate state law.

Many older Texas homes still run on 100-amp panels, and adding modern HVAC, EV chargers, or electric and induction ranges often calls for 200-amp service. If your checklist flags double-tapped breakers, federal Pacific equipment, or scorch marks, schedule a licensed TDLR electrician assessment today.
